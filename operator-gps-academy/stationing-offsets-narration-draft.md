## Scene 1 — Reading a station (visual: dozer and overhead road alignment)
**Instructor:**
“Welcome back to Operator GPS Academy. Today we're working with stationing and offsets. These numbers are how you know where you are along a road or another surveyed alignment. Think of the alignment as a measured path, not necessarily a straight line. Every point along that path has a distance from its defined start. That's the station.

“On this example job, the station shown is ten plus two-fifty. Using the metric convention in this exercise, that means ten thousand two hundred and fifty metres along the selected alignment from its defined origin. Don't assume every job uses the same convention. Some projects use different starting stations or station equations. Your approved plans tell you what the numbers mean.

“Watch the overhead view. Our dozer is near a road centreline. As it moves forward in the direction of increasing station, the station number increases. If the machine turns around and travels back, the number decreases. Stationing describes location along the alignment, not which direction the operator is facing.

“Here's the first check I want you to make before you touch the blade: Which alignment have you selected? On a large project there can be several. A road centreline, an edge of pavement, and a separate utility alignment might all have different stations at the same physical location. If you select the wrong one, a number that looks perfectly reasonable could be misleading.”

**Pause and display:** Station 10+200 → 10+225 → 10+250. Ask: “Which direction is increasing station?” Reveal arrow and explanation.

## Scene 2 — Left and right offsets (visual: plan view with 3.5-metre reference lines)
**Instructor:**
“Now let's add the second number: offset. Offset is the sideways distance from the chosen alignment to the reference point the system is reporting. The important phrase is chosen reference point. Depending on your setup, a screen may report a blade point, the machine centre or some other configured point. You must know which one you're looking at.

“For our exercise, the selected point is three and a half metres right of the centreline at station ten plus two-fifty. Right and left are defined while looking toward increasing station. They do not swap just because you turn the dozer around.

“Let's test that. The machine is facing forward along increasing station, and the point is on the right side. Now turn the dozer around without moving the point. The point is still right of the alignment, even though it may now appear on the operator's left. That catches people out when they first start reading offsets.

“Before using the offset to position a blade, confirm the selected alignment, the offset direction, and exactly where on the machine the displayed position is calculated. A blade tip can be several metres away from the machine centre. Confusing the two can put the cutting edge in the wrong place.”

**Pause and display:** Turn machine 180 degrees; freeze plan view. Ask: “Does the signed offset change?” Answer: “No, not unless the point crosses the alignment or the reference changes.”

## Scene 3 — Offset, elevation and cut/fill (visual: generic screen plus cross-section)
**Instructor:**
“Station and offset tell us where to look up the design elevation. They do not, by themselves, tell us how much to cut or fill. For that, we need the correct design surface and the measured elevation at the same reference point.

“Here's our example. At station ten plus two-fifty, three and a half metres right, the approved design elevation is one hundred and two point four five zero metres. The measured cutting-edge elevation at that same point is one hundred and two point six zero zero metres. Subtract the design from the measured elevation. The difference is positive zero point one five zero metres, or fifteen centimetres above design.

“In this lesson's convention, that means fifteen centimetres of cut remains. But don't memorize a plus or minus sign without checking the system. Displays can label cut and fill differently. Confirm the actual direction of correction against a known control point and the project procedure.

“Let's look at why the offset matters. The road centreline design elevation here is one hundred and two point five two zero metres. The crossfall drops two percent toward the right. Two percent of three and a half metres is seven centimetres. That gives us one hundred and two point four five zero metres at the right edge. If you move farther right, the target changes again. At five metres right, under the same constant slope, the target would be one hundred and two point four two zero metres.

“Never compare an elevation at the centreline with a measured blade elevation at the edge and call the difference cut or fill. Both elevations have to refer to the same physical point. If the machine's reading jumps or disagrees with a survey check, stop tolerance-critical grading and investigate. Don't chase the blade display blindly.”

**Pause and display:** (102.600 − 102.450) m = 0.150 m above design. (0.02 × 3.50) m = 0.070 m drop. Ask student to calculate target at 5 m right.
