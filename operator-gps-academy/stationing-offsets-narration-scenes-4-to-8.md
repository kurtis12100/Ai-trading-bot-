## Scene 4 — Following an alignment through a curve (approx. 4 minutes including pauses)
**Visual:** Overhead camera of curved road centreline; station ticks, tangent arrows, perpendicular offset line at nearest alignment station.
**Instructor:** “Let's make the job a little more realistic. A road doesn't stay straight forever. Watch the centreline bend to the right. Stationing still measures distance *along* the alignment. It does not measure straight-line distance between two stakes. And your left and right offset directions are referenced to the alignment at the station in question. That's why an offset line turns as the alignment curves. If you look only at the compass heading of your dozer, it's easy to misunderstand where the design expects your blade to be.

“Imagine we're grading the right shoulder. At station ten plus two-fifty, we're three and a half metres right. As we travel into the curve, we maintain the specified offset relative to the alignment—not relative to a fixed north-south direction. The machine's path will bend with the design. If your offset increases, check whether the machine is drifting away from the reference or whether you're tracking a different point on the blade.

“Here is an important distinction. Some software projects station and offset to the closest point on the alignment, while others use particular design features or configured references. Don't assume the geometry or labels. Check the approved job setup and what the machine actually reports. If the plan view looks strange or the reference line suddenly changes, stop and verify rather than steering by a number you don't understand.”
**Exercise:** Freeze a curve. Show a point at +3.5 m right. Ask learner to point toward increasing station and identify the local right side.

## Scene 5 — Cross-section and changing grade (approx. 4 minutes)
**Visual:** Animated roadway cross-section with centreline 102.520 m; -2% crossfall to right; right offset marker moves from 0 to 3.5 m to 5 m.
**Instructor:** “Now we're going to connect the horizontal position to the vertical design. A road surface can change elevation across its width. At our example station, the centreline design is one hundred and two point five two zero metres. To the right, the design drops two percent. A two-percent crossfall means a drop of two centimetres for every metre horizontally. At three and a half metres right, that is seven centimetres lower than centreline: one hundred and two point four five zero metres.

“Move the marker to five metres right. That's a ten-centimetre drop, so the design there is one hundred and two point four two zero metres. This is why a good operator doesn't treat a single elevation as the target everywhere. The design target belongs to a specific position.

“Now add a longitudinal grade. If the road rises as station increases, the centreline elevation at the next station will also change. To find the correct blade target, you need the design at your current station and offset. Don't carry yesterday's elevation to a new station. The model should calculate it, but the operator must still understand enough to spot a mismatch.

“Real roads may have crowns, superelevation, transition zones, variable widths, and separate layers. Our constant two-percent example is only a teaching exercise. In a real job, use the approved surface and independent checks.”
**Exercise:** Ask learner to calculate design elevation at 4 m right: 102.440 m.

## Scene 6 — Cut/fill sign and blade reference (approx. 4 minutes)
**Visual:** Blade side elevation and close-up generic screen; two marked points on cutting edge.
**Instructor:** “Look closely at the cutting edge. Where exactly is the displayed elevation being measured? Some configurations report a selected point or blade edge, and a tilt can make the left and right ends different elevations. A single number doesn't necessarily describe every part of the blade.

“At our chosen point, measured elevation is one hundred and two point six zero zero metres. Design at that same point is one hundred and two point four five zero metres. The difference is plus zero point one five zero metres. That point is fifteen centimetres high. Under our lesson's convention, fifteen centimetres of cut remains. If the measured point were one hundred and two point four zero zero, it would be five centimetres below design, meaning fill is required under the same convention.

“Always check the actual display's cut/fill convention. The colour or sign alone isn't proof. And don't move the blade automatically just because a number changes. A wrong surface, incorrect offset, lost corrections, loose sensor or calibration error can create a plausible but wrong instruction.

“Before the pass, confirm the approved design, reference point, machine setup, positioning quality and site control checks. Keep attention on people, utilities and changing ground conditions. Machine guidance is a tool, not a substitute for judgement.”
**Exercise:** Measured 102.400 m versus design 102.450 m at same point: answer 0.050 m below design.

## Scene 7 — Guided dozer pass and discrepancy drill (approx. 4 minutes)
**Visual:** Simulated dozer approaches marked grading area; screen transitions 0.15 m high → 0.08 m high → 0.02 m high → near target. Cutaway reveals wrong surface selection in second take.
**Instructor:** “Let's follow a controlled grading pass. We're tracking the approved alignment and the selected blade point. The measured elevation begins fifteen centimetres above the design. As the pass progresses, the difference reduces. Watch the terrain as well as the display. We don't want a machine that looks accurate on screen but is working against the wrong model.

“Now let's introduce a fault. The screen shows zero, but the survey check says the surface is ten centimetres high. What do we do? We stop tolerance-critical work and investigate. Check that the design file and revision are correct. Confirm no temporary surface offset is active. Compare the coordinate reference and known site control. Check machine geometry and positioning status according to the approved procedures. Document what you find and involve the survey or machine-control specialist as required.

“Never hide a discrepancy by guessing at a calibration value. That can make one point look correct while shifting the whole job. The safe result is a verified reference and a clear explanation of the difference.”
**Exercise:** Pick the correct first action: stop and verify, not increase speed or alter the datum.

## Scene 8 — Final practical assessment and close (approx. 3 minutes)
**Visual:** Four stations on a road corridor, left/right offset markers, cross-section and cut/fill screen. Pause after each question.
**Instructor:** “Before we finish, I want you to talk through a field scenario. You're at station ten plus two-fifty, three and a half metres right of centreline. The design elevation at your selected cutting-edge point is one hundred and two point four five zero metres. The measured elevation at that same point is one hundred and two point six zero zero metres. What's your cut? Fifteen centimetres, using our stated convention.

“Now turn the dozer around. Does right offset become left? No. Left and right still refer to increasing station.

“Next, the road falls two percent toward the right. If the centreline is one hundred and two point five two zero, what's the design at five metres right? One hundred and two point four two zero metres.

“Finally, your display says on grade but an independent check disagrees. Don't keep pushing. Stop, verify the design, positioning and reference, and resolve the mismatch.

“If you can explain those decisions without guessing, you're building the judgement that makes machine control useful. The display gives you information; you remain responsible for the operation. In the next lesson we'll apply these skills to a longer cut-and-fill exercise.”
**Assessment:** Four questions above, then recap station, offset, reference point, design elevation, measured elevation, verification.
